## Overview
The T'boli live in the highlands around **Lake Sebu** in South Cotabato, Mindanao. They are known across the world for **t'nalak**, a cloth woven from abaca fiber in rich browns, reds and creams. T'boli culture is famous for its beadwork, brass casting and music, and for striking traditional dress worn during celebrations.

## Myths & Stories
T'boli oral literature includes the epic of **Todbulul**, a hero whose adventures are chanted over many nights. In traditional belief, the supreme being is often named **D'wata**.

The designs of t'nalak are said to come to weavers in **dreams**, given by **Fu Dalu**, the spirit of the abaca. A weaver does not simply invent a pattern. She receives it, remembers it and ties it into the fiber, thread by thread. For this reason T'boli weavers are often called *dreamweavers*.

> A weaver does not invent the pattern. She dreams it.

## Music
T'boli music features the **hegelung**, a long two-stringed lute, and the **kumbing**, a bamboo jaw harp. Ensembles of bossed gongs called **klintang** are also played. Instrumental music can imitate speech and courtship, and some melodies are said to "talk".

## Beliefs & Rituals
Traditional T'boli belief honors a world filled with spirits of the forest, the water and the abaca. Before and during weaving, weavers traditionally observe customs and taboos, as the work is considered spiritual as well as artistic. Rituals mark planting, harvest and life's turning points.

## Crafts
**Lang Dulay** (1924–2015), a master t'nalak weaver, received the **Gawad sa Manlilikha ng Bayan** (National Living Treasures Award) in 1998. She was known for keeping more than 100 designs in memory. T'boli artisans are also known for **lost-wax brass casting**, producing bells, figurines and belts, and for intricate **beadwork** in earrings, combs and necklaces.

Weaving t'nalak takes weeks to months. The abaca is stripped, knotted, tie-dyed by hand and finally beaten and polished with a cowrie shell until it shines.
