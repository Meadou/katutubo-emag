## Overview
The Ifugao are an Indigenous People of the Cordillera highlands of northern Luzon. Their name is often explained as coming from *i-pugo*, "people of the earth" or "people of the hill". For centuries, and some say far longer (scholars still debate the dates), Ifugao families have carved the mountainsides into terraces. They are among the most famous landscapes on Earth.

The **Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras** were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1995. They are not ruins. They are a living system of irrigation, forest stewardship (*muyong*) and shared labor that is still tended today.

> The terraces are not just fields; they are the record of generations who listened to the mountain.

## Myths & Stories
Ifugao oral tradition holds the **Hudhud**, long chanted narratives performed during rice harvesting, wakes and other community occasions. A lead chanter sings the verses and a chorus answers. The stories follow ancestral heroes such as **Aliguyon**, celebrating bravery, kinship and the value of the land. The Hudhud was proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2001.

Another widely told story recounts a great flood. Siblings **Wigan and Bugan** survived on a mountain peak and became ancestors of the people. Retellings differ from village to village, which is part of what makes them alive.

## Music
Ifugao music is closely tied to ritual and community. Flat gongs (**gangsa**) are played in ensembles during celebrations. The rhythm interlocks between players rather than following one leader. Other instruments include the bamboo nose flute, bamboo zithers and the **bangibang**, a curved hardwood percussion bar.

## Beliefs & Rituals
Traditional Ifugao belief recognizes a vast world of deities, ancestor spirits and nature spirits. The **mumbaki** are ritual specialists who perform prayers, recitations and offerings at key moments: planting, harvest, illness, marriage and death.

The **bulul** are carved wooden figures associated with the rice granary and the abundance of the harvest. To many Ifugao they are sacred, not decorative. Their meaning comes from ritual, so we ask readers to see them with respect rather than as souvenirs.

## Crafts
Ifugao woodcarving is renowned, from granary figures to bowls, spoons and the prestigious **hagabi**, a massive carved bench owned by families of high status. Weaving traditions produce distinctive blankets and wrap-around skirts in deep reds, blacks and whites. Today, community-based tourism and cooperatives help artisans keep these crafts alive on their own terms.
