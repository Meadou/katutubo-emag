## Overview
**Mangyan** is the collective name for the Indigenous Peoples of Mindoro. It covers at least eight groups, each with its own language and customs: the **Iraya, Alangan, Tadyawan, Tau-buid, Bangon, Buhid, Hanunuo** and **Ratagnon**. Living mainly in the island's interior uplands, Mangyan communities are known for farming, weaving and one of the Philippines' most remarkable literary traditions.

## Myths & Stories
The Hanunuo Mangyan compose the **ambahan**, a poem built from lines of **seven syllables**. Ambahan are chanted, often in courtship, giving advice or welcoming visitors. Many speak indirectly through images of nature: a bird, a vine, the moon.

Ambahan were traditionally **inscribed on bamboo** with a knife tip. The poems are written in **Surat Mangyan**, an Indigenous script descended from pre-colonial Philippine writing systems. The Hanunuo and Buhid scripts are still taught and used today. Philippine paleographs including these scripts were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 1999.

> Seven syllables a line, carved into bamboo, carried for generations.

## Music
Hanunuo Mangyan music includes the **gitgit**, a small bowed fiddle, and the **kudyapi**, a plucked lute, along with bamboo flutes and jaw harps. Songs are frequently paired with ambahan, so music and poetry are woven together.

## Beliefs & Rituals
Mangyan belief traditions vary by group. Many honor a supreme being alongside spirits of nature and of ancestors. Among the Hanunuo, rituals for the dead, including secondary burial practices, hold deep importance. Respect for the forest and the land runs throughout Mangyan life.

## Crafts
Hanunuo weavers create indigo-dyed cloth and embroider garments with a cross-shaped motif known as **pakudos**. Basketry is especially fine, with tightly woven baskets made from nito vine and bamboo. The **Mangyan Heritage Center** in Calapan works with communities to preserve and teach Surat Mangyan and the ambahan.
