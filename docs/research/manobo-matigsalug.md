## Manobo overview

**Who they are**
- "Manobo" is a broad name for many related peoples spread across a large part of Mindanao. It does not name one single tribe [6][7].
- The NCCA profile gives a 1994 population of 749,042 for the Manobo including subgroups. It also warns that "the various subgroupings are not sufficiently defined" [7 *(snippet)*][6].
- Manobo groups live from Sarangani to Agusan del Sur, in the Davao provinces, Bukidnon, and North and South Cotabato. They live in many kinds of places, from rugged highland to coast [6].
- Traditional leadership rests with a *datu*, a skilled leader whose standing comes from what he does [6]. (The Matigsalug describe their own datu below.)

**The name (endonym)**
- One common reading is that *Manobo* comes from *Manuvu*, meaning "people" [6].
- Garvan (1931) gave several possible derivations: "people"; *man* + *suba* ("river people"); or "robust/numerous people," from a 1600s friar's account. He said he could not confirm any of them [5]. **Treat the meaning as uncertain.**
- Garvan also saw that outsiders used "Manobo" loosely, even to mean "unbaptised." Some groups preferred their own place-based names, such as *Agusnon* [5]. This is one reason to use each people's own name.

**Do not over-generalise. These are distinct peoples:**
- The NCCA classification, as reproduced by the C.E. Smith Museum [6], lists these subgroups:
  - **Ata subgroup:** Dugbatang, Talaingod, Tagauanum.
  - **Bagobo subgroup:** Attaw/Jangan/Klata/Obo/Giangan, Eto (Ata), Kailawan, Langilan, Manuvu/Obo, **Matigsalug (Matig Salug)**, Tagaluro, Tigdapaya.
  - **Higaonon subgroup:** Agusan, Lanao, Misamis.
  - **North Cotabato:** **Ilianen, Livunganen**, Pulenyan.
  - **South Cotabato:** Cotabato (with Tasaday and Blit), Sarangani, Tagabawa.
  - **Western Bukidnon:** Kiriyeteka, Ilentungen, Pulangiyen.
  - Also listed: Agusan del Sur, Banwaon, Bukidnon [6].
- Linguists group the Manobo *languages* differently [22]. Elkins (1974) has a Northern branch (Kagayanen, Kinamigin, Binukid) and a Southern branch (Tagabawa, Sarangani, Cotabato/Tasaday). His Western branch has Obo, Ilianen, Western Bukidnon and Livunganen. His Eastern branch has Agusan and Dibabawon, and his Central branch has Ata and **Matigsalug (Tigwa)**.
- Hans Brandeis's music research also names, among others, the Umayamnon, Dulangan, Blit, Tigwa, Ata, Arumanen, Kulamanen and Tinananon Manobo [3].
- **Note:** Some sources place the Matigsalug *under* the Manobo. Others call them "a distinct subgroup from the Manobos" [18]. The NCCA files them under "Bagobo" [6]. The classification itself is still debated.

## Matigsalug

**Name and meaning**
- In their own documentation the Matigsalug write: "Our tribal name **Matigsalog** is a give away of our origin. It means 'resident from the Salug River' (Davao River) specifically along its watershed" [1].
- Other spellings: *Matigsalug*, *Matig Salug*, *Matigsalog* [1][6].
- SunStar breaks the name into *matig* ("place of origin" or "from") and *salug/salog* ("river") [16][17].
- After the 1975 resistance led by Datu Gawilan, the name came to mean the whole tribe across Bukidnon, Cotabato and Davao [1].

**Location**
- The Matigsalug write that, according to SIL, people in Panganan said "the true Matigsalog" live in this area [1]:
  - north: Kalanganan;
  - east: up to the Lasang River watershed (not including it);
  - south: Gumitan;
  - southwest: Kulaman River, North Cotabato;
  - west: Sinuda.
- Places named in news reports [16][17]:
  - **Davao City:** Marilog (3rd district), Paquibato, Tala-ingod, Calinan.
  - **Bukidnon:** Kitaotao, San Fernando.
  - **Cotabato:** Arakan.
  - They live along the banks and tributaries of the Salug, which is now called the Davao River.
- A CMU study did its fieldwork in **Sinuda, Kitaotao, Bukidnon** [2]. Wikipedia names the "Tigwa-Salug Valley of San Fernando, Bukidnon" [18].

**Approximate coordinates for a 3D map**
- **The Matigsalug's own source:** the domain area they describe (bordered by San Fernando to the north, Davao City and part of South Cotabato to the south, Davao del Norte to the east, and Quezon, Kibawe and part of Kitaotao to the west) is "between the latitudes 7 45 00 and longitudes of 125 05 00 and 125 25 00." The highest point is about 1,300 m, the land is rugged, and the national highway to Davao City runs through it [1, quoting PENRO-DENR].
- **Town reference points** [28]:
  - Kitaotao poblacion: 7.639° N, 125.007° E.
  - San Fernando, Bukidnon: 7.917° N, 125.329° E.
  - Marilog barangay, Davao City: 7.282° N, 125.307° E (this is a barangay point, not the centre of the district).
- **Suggested marker for the homeland, my own estimate:** about **7.6° N, 125.2° E**. This is not a sourced value; it is only for placing a pin on the map.
- **Barangays in the area** [1]: Sumalili, Kulaman Valley, Baganihan, Mantakil, Panganan, Digongan, Kiulum, Kipilis, Sinuda, Tawas, Napaalico, East and West Dalurong, White Kulaman, Kabalanti-an, Kahawayan, Sagundanon, Gulatid, Buda, Saluman. Many of these match present-day Kitaotao barangays [28].
- **The river:** Wikipedia lists the Salug River as one source of the Davao River, rising at San Fernando, Bukidnon [31]. Some sources say the Salug *is* the Davao River [1][16][17]. Others call it a tributary [18]. See *Uncertain*.

**Language**
- Their language is Matigsalug Manobo, a Manobo language [23][22].
- Wikipedia lists four dialects: Kulamanen, Tigwa, Tala Ingod, and Matigsalug proper [23].
- SIL has worked in the community for decades and published a grammar [18][1].
- The CMU study warns that the language is under pressure from language shift [2]. Many people also speak Cebuano and Filipino [18].

**Ancestral domain (CADT)**
- **Area:** 102,324.8186 hectares, covering parts of Bukidnon, Davao City and the Arakan Valley in North Cotabato [19 *(snippet)*][18].
- **Date of issue:** 31 October 2003 according to [19 *(snippet)*][18]. A PSA registry entry gives CADT No. **R10-KIT-0703-011**, dated **25 July 2003** and registered in Bukidnon in 2006 [21 *(snippet)*]. **The two dates conflict.**
- **Areas covered, per the PSA entry:** parts of Marilog District (Davao City), Arakan (North Cotabato), and Kitaotao, Quezon, Kibawe and San Fernando (Bukidnon) [21 *(snippet)*].
- **Holder:** linked to the **Federation of Matigsalug-Manobo Tribal Councils** [20 *(snippet)*][2].
- **Today:** a Matigsalug member was sworn in as Indigenous Peoples Mandatory Representative (IPMR) to the Davao City council in 2022 [30 *(snippet)*].

**Land and leadership, in their own words** [1]
- "As a people we do not comprehend land ownership – only stewardship as a sign of respect to the rightful owners – the spirits."
- "The word boundary has no equivalent term in the Matigsalog language."
- A village is made up of clans, each headed by a datu. A datu is believed to be **born** with special abilities, not elected. A supreme datu makes the final decision when ordinary datus cannot agree.
- A good datu is "honest and sincere… hospitable… even-tempered," and works his own field.
- In 1975 the Matigsalug resisted loggers, ranchers and land encroachment. The resistance lasted two months and two weeks and was led by Datu Lorenzo Gawilan [1].

**Livelihood**
- They have farmed swidden fields "since time immemorial." A datu leads the communal planting, and half of the harvest goes to the communal store (*lulatong*) [1].
- They also hunt, fish, and keep home gardens. They write that home gardens came from other groups, and that "homegarden is the forest" [1].
- Today they grow rice, cash crops, vegetables and fruit [16]. They are "river people who fish and farm" [17].

## Story (epic)

**Which story, and whose it is**
- **The Ulahingan** belongs to the **Livunganen-Arumanen Manobo** of North Cotabato. It is "traditionally told through a **sacred chant** performed by a *tala-ulahingan* (chanter)" [8].
  - The name comes from *ulahing*, "to sing and chant." It is chanted over several days and has 79 episodes of 4,000–6,000 lines each [11].
  - It tells of creation, "the life and exploits of **Agyu** and his people before reaching **Nelendengan** (paradise) and their subsequent life in a paradise on earth" [8][9].
  - Dr. Elena Maquiso of Silliman University began recording it in 1963 with chanter Jose Silay. The six-volume *Ulahingan Series* followed. The NCCA published a Filipino translation in 2021 [8][9]. The unpublished *kepu'unpu'un* part tells of the migration of Agyu and his clan [10].
- **The Agyu epic of the Ilianen (Ilianon) Manobo** of North Cotabato is a closely related telling. It is 1,279 lines in seven episodes. E. Arsenio Manuel recorded it in 1963 from the singer Blagtas Pandakan [12][13].
- **The link to the Matigsalug**
  - Agyu is described as a common ancestor of several peoples, including the **Matigsalugs**, Talaandig, Kulamanen, Ilianen, Higaonon and Livunganen-Arumanen [15 *(snippet)*].
  - Matigsalug prayers (*panubad*) recorded in 2024 call on ancestors (*kaapapuan*) with the **same names as Agyu's family in the epics**. They include Apu Tulalang, Apu Banlak, Apu Beybayan, Apu Mungan, Apu Lene, Apu Pamulew and Apu Ikwangan. The prayers say these ancestors "ride on the *salimbal*," that they went up to heaven, and that "they are not perceived as dead" [2].
- **No Matigsalug-specific published version of the Agyu story was found.** The beats below follow the published Ilianen Agyu text [12][13] and end with the Ulahingan's paradise [8][14]. They should be credited to those peoples. You can say the Matigsalug honour the same ancestors, but do not call it "the Matigsalug epic."
- **"Ulaging" / "Olaging":** the Ulaging is described as an epic cycle of the **Talaandig Manobo of Bukidnon** [24]. *Olaging* is also used more generally for epic stories kept by a *balyan* (shown for the Talaingod Manobo) [25]. No source tied the word to the Matigsalug.
- **Tulalang / Tuwaang:** an epic hero of the Manuvu, Obo and Talaingod traditions [25][29]. A 2024 UP Mindanao study suggests linked hero traditions ("Tuwaang and Tolalang") around Arakan and **San Fernando, Bukidnon**, "a cultural corridor of linked epic traditions" [29].

> **Important.** The Matigsalug name Banlak, Mungan, Lene/Lono, Pamulew, Ikwangan and Beybayan as **ancestors they pray to** [2]. Showing them as statues may be sensitive. See **Do not depict**. A safe default is to show only **Agyu, Kuyasu, the people, and Tanagyaw** as figures, as anonymous and stylised as possible, and to show the honoured ancestors as light or silhouettes. Ask the community first.

### Beat 1: The beeswax quarrel at Ayuman
- **Setting:** Ayuman, the homeland, beside a river with forest, where beeswax is gathered [12][13].
- **Characters:** Agyu; his brothers Kuyasu and Banlak; their father Pamulaw (named, but he does not act in this beat); a downriver datu [13].
- **Action:**
  - Agyu sends nine lumps of beeswax to a Moro datu, carried by Kuyasu and Banlak.
  - The datu thinks it too little and throws it at Kuyasu, hurting his sore foot. Kuyasu kills the datu.
  - Banlak runs home to warn Agyu [12][13].
- **Animation note:** show the beeswax being thrown and the brothers running home. Do not show the killing, and do not cast Muslims/Moro people as villains. Narrate it as "a quarrel over trade with a downriver datu" and add a line of context.

### Beat 2: The fort on Mount Ilian
- **Setting:** Mount Ilian, near the Pulangi River in the text [13].
- **Characters:** Agyu, his people, attacking warriors [12][13].
- **Action:**
  - Agyu foresees war and leads his people up Ilian, where they build a fort.
  - They gather logs and boulders tied with rattan. When the attackers climb, Agyu has the rattan cut and the logs roll down [12][13].
- **Animation note:** logs rolling down a slope and figures pulling back. No bodies.

### Beat 3: Honey and healing at Pinamatun
- **Setting:** Mount Pinamatun. Agyu hunts on Mount Sandawa [12][13].
- **Characters:** Agyu; Lono' (also Lena/Lene); the sisters Yambungan and Ikwangan; Banlak; Mungan, Banlak's sick wife [13].
- **Action:**
  - The sisters are stung by bees, and Lono' finds the hives.
  - Agyu returns with a wild pig, and the meat and honey are shared.
  - Banlak will not take food to Mungan, so Lono' carries her share to her and finds her **healed**.
  - She gives betel nut and young rice to share [12][13].
- **Animation note:** this is the gentlest beat for figures, centred on sharing food. Mungan is an honoured ancestor to the Matigsalug [2], so show her as a glow or silhouette inside the house.

### Beat 4: Mungan goes up to the skyworld
- **Setting:** back at Ayuman, then the mouth of the Tigyandang [12][13].
- **Characters:** Agyu, the people, Mungan (shown only as light) [12].
- **Action:** when the people return, Mungan is gone. The summary says she has gone up to the skyworld. The people move on to Tigyandang [12]. (The ascent is in the summary of [12] and is not stated in the episode text of [13].)
- **Animation note:** a column of light rising from the house. **No figure.** For the Matigsalug, the ancestors who rose on the *salimbal* are honoured and "not dead" [2].

### Beat 5: Tanagyaw, the boy defender
- **Setting:** Tigyandang and the bay of Linayagon, later Baklayon and Sunglawon [12][13].
- **Characters:** Tanagyaw (Agyu's youngest son); Agyu; Buy'anon and Paniguan (daughters of other datus); invaders [13].
- **Action:**
  - Tanagyaw, still a boy, holds off the invaders.
  - He turns down a marriage offer from Buy'anon because he is still a boy.
  - Later he helps Baklayon, and Paniguan follows him home. Agyu accepts their marriage and gives them Sunglawon [12][13].
- **Animation note:** a boy with a shield and spear standing at the shore. Keep the fighting abstract. You can skip this beat to stay short.

### Beat 6 (optional ending, from the Ulahingan): Nalandangan / Nelendangan
- **Setting:** Nalandangan (also Nelendengan), a fortress-paradise by the sea. In one published episode the people "arrived from Aruman on a huge ship" [14]. The Ulahingan as a whole tells of their life in "a paradise on earth" [8].
- **Characters:** Agyu and his people. In [14], the warriors guarding its forts include Kuyasu, Banlak/Vanlak and Agyu's sons Seyluwen and Nebeyaw [14].
- **Action:** a great vessel carries the people to a shining city [14][8].
- **Animation note:** show the vessel and the city as light, and the people as a small, faceless crowd. The *salimbal* named in Matigsalug prayers may be the same kind of sky vessel [2]. That link is my guess; confirm it.

## Music

> Any sound in the e-magazine is **synthesised and labelled "not a traditional recording."** Physical details come from the sources named. **Group attribution matters:** most detailed descriptions are of *neighbouring* Manobo groups (Agusan, Tigwa, Ata), not of the Matigsalug themselves.

### Kuglung: two-string boat lute (Matigsalug name, per [3])
- **Group:**
  - Brandeis lists the **Matigsalug Manobo** in the "kuglung complex" with the Ata and Tigwa, who "all call their instruments *kuglung*" [3].
  - One photo shows Casiano Olandag (kuglung, **Matigsalug Manobo**) playing with Gina Manyawron (salurey, Ata Manobo) [3].
  - Other names: *kudlung* (Umayamnon, some Agusan), *kudiyung* (Agusan), *kutiyapi* (Western Bukidnon, **Arumanen**), *katiyapi* (Talaandig) [3]. *Kudyapi* is not the Matigsalug term.
- **Shape and materials:**
  - Carved from **one solid log**, with the body hollowed from the back and closed with a thin board [3].
  - Medium-sized. The lower body is box-shaped and blends into a long, thin neck of even width, with a narrowed "waist" where they meet [3].
  - A **horse or monitor-lizard head** is carved at the lower end, and the head is hook-shaped [3].
  - For scale: Philippine boat lutes measure 90–250 cm in general [3]. Garvan measured an Agusan *kudlung* at 1.5–2 m [5].
- **Strings:**
  - Two strings: a **melody string with frets** and a **drone string without frets** [3].
  - Today the strings are steel (guitar or fishing wire). Earlier they were vine, hemp or rattan [3]. Garvan records vine-core strings [5].
  - Matigsalug, Ata and related groups fine-tune the melody string with **rattan rings** looped around the neck [3].
- **Frets:** lumps of **black beeswax** with bamboo, coconut-shell, fern-wood or buffalo-horn inserts [3]. Garvan counted 9–12 frets [5].
- **How it is played:**
  - A plectrum is tied to the index or middle finger. The main stroke is **upward** [3].
  - The left hand stops the melody string. Hammer-ons and pull-offs make soft clicks, and the thumb can tap the soundboard like a drum [3].
- **Who plays:**
  - **Men.** In Matigsalug, Ata and Tigwa practice it is often paired with a woman on the saluray, who usually sings. They sing stanzas in turn and dance side by side [3].
  - In Agusan in the early 1900s, Garvan said it was not played by women and not used to accompany song [5]. This is a regional difference.
- **Scales:** solos use an older **hemitonic** scale (*kalindaan*) or a newer **anhemitonic** one (*baligen*). Duets always use *baligen* [3].
- **Synth idea:** a plucked, bright melody string over a steady drone, using an anhemitonic pentatonic scale. Label it as an approximation.

### Saluray / salurey: bamboo tube zither
- **Group:** played with the kuglung by the Ata, **Matigsalug**, Tigwa and related groups [3]. The Field Museum holds two bamboo tube zithers named *saloday* from the **Kulaman Manobo** [26]. (Kulamanen is listed as a Matigsalug dialect [23].)
- **What it is:** a **polychordal bamboo tube zither** [3]. Its strings are cut from the bamboo's own skin and stay attached at both ends, which makes it an idiochord.
- **Physical detail, from a similar Agusan Manobo zither in Garvan [5]:**
  - It is made from one internode of large bamboo.
  - Five thin strips are cut along the surface and lifted with small wooden wedges at each end, which also set the tuning. Two bass strings sit on one side and three treble strings on the other, about 3 cm apart.
  - A long slit between them and holes in the node walls make it louder.
- **How it is played:** held in both hands and plucked with fingers of both hands [5].
- **Who plays:**
  - **Usually women** in Matigsalug, Ata and Tigwa duets, often singing [3].
  - Garvan says men and women both played the Agusan zither [5].
- **Synth idea:** short, dry plucks with a quick decay and a bamboo tube resonance.

### Kubing: bamboo jaw harp
- **Group:** recorded among the Agusan Manobo [5]. Shared by the Bukidnon and Tigwahanon (Tigwa) under the name *kubing/kebing* [4 *(summary only)*]. A Matigsalug-specific source was not found.
- **Shape:** a slender bamboo strip about **26 cm long**. A tongue about **6 cm** long is cut from the middle and stays attached at one end, with a small cavity behind it [5].
- **How it is played:**
  - Held to the lips. The player taps a needle-like spur at the end to make the tongue vibrate, and changes the shape of the mouth to make the tones [5].
  - Brandeis reports it can carry **coded spoken messages** that an experienced listener can decode word for word [4 *(summary only)*].
- **Synth idea:** a buzzy drone whose overtones move in a "wah" pattern.

### Agung: bossed gong
- **Group:** the "most widely distributed" brass instrument among Mindanao Lumad [27]. Garvan describes it for the Agusan Manobo [5]. **No Matigsalug-specific source was found.**
- **What it is:** a bronze or brass gong with a raised central knob (boss). Agusan Manobo gongs were small imported gongs bought from Visayan traders [5].
- **How it is played:** hung up or held in the hand and struck on the **knob** with a piece of wood. It keeps time with the drum during dances [5].
- **Sacred note:** "Both the drum and gong have a certain religious character". They were used in major religious celebrations and kept in the priest's house [5].
- **Synth idea:** a low, ringing strike with a long decay.

### Drum
- **Group:** the Agusan Manobo, early 1900s [5]. The local name was not captured, and "tambol" was **not** verified for the Matigsalug.
- **Shape and materials:**
  - A section of palm trunk about **25 cm high and 20 cm across**, with **deerskin** heads at both ends [5].
  - The heads are tightened by forcing rattan rings down the sides, then warmed over the fire before playing [5].
- **How it is played:** the left hand taps one head for the "bass" beat and the right hand plays a bamboo slat. There are 20–50 named rhythms, many imitating animals [5].
- **Who plays:** "men, women, and children" [5].

### Kulintang: probably leave out
- A Wikipedia photo caption mentions a "Matigsalug kulintang ensemble" at Kaamulan 2007 [18]. No real source backs this up. Kulintang gong-row music is mainly associated with other peoples, so **leave it out unless a Matigsalug source confirms it.**

## Beliefs & rituals

> Kept short on purpose. These are living, sacred practices.

- **Manama:**
  - In their own words: "We believe that **Manama** whom you call God occupies the highest position in the hierarchy of the supernatural." They compare the other unseen powers, "powerful but kind when respected," to vice presidents and cabinet secretaries [1].
  - In prayer, Manama is called *Amey ney ne Manama*, "Our Father Manama" [2].
- **Magbebaye:**
  - In the 2024 study, *Magbebaye* is a **female** deity, second after Manama, and addressed as "mother" in prayer [2].
  - This differs from other Bukidnon groups, where *Magbabaya* is the highest, male deity [2].
  - SunStar says Manama gave the Matigsalug the "Magbavaye" to follow for self-governance [16]. This reads differently from [2]; see *Uncertain*.
- **Guardian spirits:** *tahikbal* or *tahabikal* guard people, datus and the environment. They live in *butelakan*, where Manama resides [2].
- **Spirits in nature:**
  - Spirits are present in "rocks, water, trees, flowers." "We believe that the forest is a sacred place." "Trees, water resources all belong to the spirits, we are but stewards" [1].
  - Forests are sanctuaries and hunting is limited: only boars are hunted, and sows and young are spared [1].
- **Panubad (ritual prayer):** central to almost all activities. It is performed before tilling the land, at weddings and burials, for healing, and to settle disputes. A **datu or a baylan** leads it [2].
  - Offerings include white, black and red chickens, each with its own meaning [2].
  - Another offering is *inapuhan*: betel leaf (*manika*), areca nut (*mamaen*) and lime (*apug*). Tobacco can take its place [2].
  - A one-peso coin may also be offered [2].
- **Farming rite:** before swidden work, the datu leads ***sunggod te kamangga***, "to feed the whetstone," where the tools are "fed." It shows reverence for "the spirits who own the land, forests and the farm implements" [1].
- **Healer:** the *tumanuron* (traditional medicine man) mediates with an offended spirit. He learns through sleep what caused the illness, and his rites can free a person's captured spirit [1].
- **Everyday taboos the community describes** [1]:
  - Twilight is a critical hour, so stay indoors.
  - Do not laugh at night.
  - Never say the names of bad spirits.
  - Do not throw things into water, trees or rocks.

## Crafts

- **Men's clothing** [1]:
  - Knee-length tight pants and a long-sleeved coat shirt, "decorated all-over with multi-colored cloth tape painstakingly sewn" on the bodice and sleeves. The same design runs down the pants to the knee.
  - A datu wears a beaded or tie-dyed turban fringed with goat's hair, a beaded waistband, and a sling bag with goat's hair and beads.
  - The *kampilan* sword marks a datu's rank.
- **Women's clothing** [1]: red, orange, blue and yellow are the main colours. They wear a plaid knee-length skirt and a waist-length blouse with elbow-length bell sleeves.
- **Ornaments** [1]:
  - Combs hung with beads or yarn balls.
  - The ***sul-oy***, a necklace with earplugs attached.
  - Brass or shell bracelets.
  - ***Tikus***, plant-fibre leg rings worn below the knee by men and women, said to give walking endurance.
  - Anklets with hawkbells.
- **Costume names given at Kadayawan** [17]:
  - *Sangkad* (headdress), *Sol-oy* (earrings), *Binularan* (blouse), *Ampit* (skirt), *Tangod* (belt).
  - Another article lists more garment names (*bandera, sinalapid, tangkulo, benanos, balaren, sangked, tekos, bali-eg, babat, sal-ey, tenuhol*) [16]. Their meanings are not given.
- **Blacksmithing (*panday*)** [1]:
  - The craft is now rare. Work starts with a ritual to Manama and the spirits who own the tools, with offerings of white chicken, betel and tobacco.
  - **Twin bamboo bellows** (*suluban*), about 1 m high, are pumped with sticks tipped with chicken feathers (*orisi*).
  - Smiths use a hammer (*landaan*). Iron today comes from old car leaf springs (*mulyi*); earlier it was traded from the Moros or from Davao.
  - They make farm tools, the kampilan, rings and bangles.
- **Weaving:** the community writes that the smithing craft, "like the fate of **Matigsalog fabric weaving**," may be "forever snuffed out" [1]. Traditional cloth weaving seems to be lost or nearly lost; check this with the community.
- **Baskets and bags:** rattan woven bags and baskets are sold at Kadayawan. Some items are made by the tribe and some are bought in the city [17].

## Do not depict

**Never show as figures** (show as light only, or not at all):
- **Manama**, the highest being [1][2].
- **Magbebaye / Magbabaya** [2].
- ***Tahikbal / tahabikal*** guardian spirits [2], ***tumanod*** spirits [1], and any nature spirits of the forest, rocks or water [1].

**Honoured ancestors (*apu / kaapapuan*): ask before showing.**
- Apu Tulalang, Apu Banlak, Apu Beybayan, Apu Mungan, Apu Lene, Apu Mahuralu, Apu Pamulew, Apu Ikwangan and others are **prayed to** in panubad [2].
- They share names with characters in the Agyu and Ulahingan epics. **Do not make realistic statues of them.** Use light or anonymous silhouettes unless the community approves.

**Real people:**
- **Datu Lorenzo Gawilan** (the 1975 leader) is a real, revered person, and is invoked in ritual as "Apu" [1][2]. Do not portray him.
- Do not use photos or names of living leaders without consent.

**Rituals and sacred sound:**
- Do not re-enact panubad. Do not show chicken offerings, betel offerings or a baylan or tumanuron in ritual as game props.
- Do not quote prayer texts as decoration.
- The **Ulahingan chant is sacred** [8]. Do not imitate chanting with synthesised "voices"; retell it in plain narration and credit the source.

**Violence and stereotypes:**
- Do not show the killings in the Agyu story.
- Avoid casting Moro or Muslim peoples as villains.

**The *salimbal* (sky vessel):**
- It is tied to ancestors who went to heaven [2]. Show it only as an abstract shape of light.

## Uncertain / needs verification

1. **NCCA content.** ncca.gov.ph could not be reached. The NCCA population (749,042, 1994) and the subgroup list come from a museum page that copies them [6] and from search snippets [7]. Check them directly with NCCA.
2. **CADT details.**
   - The area (102,324.8186 ha) and the 31 Oct 2003 date come from MindaNews and Wikipedia snippets [19][18].
   - The CADT number R10-KIT-0703-011 and the 25 July 2003 date come from a PSA snippet [21].
   - **The dates conflict.** Confirm with NCIP Region X (Bukidnon) or Region XI.
3. **The Salug and Davao rivers.** Is the Salug the Davao River itself, its upper course, or a tributary? Sources differ [1][16][18][31].
4. **Population.** The community document mentions "many of the 20,000 Matigsalog" [1]. Wikipedia gives 30,176 (2010 census) [18]. Other web figures vary widely.
5. **Classification.** The Matigsalug are filed under "Bagobo" by NCCA [6], under "Central Manobo" by linguists [22], and as distinct from the Manobo by others [18]. Tigwa is treated as a dialect by some [23] and as a separate people by others [3].
6. **Agyu and the Matigsalug.** No published Matigsalug telling was found. The claim that Agyu is their ancestor rests on a search snippet [15]. The match between epic names and the apu in Matigsalug prayers comes from [2], but I matched the names myself (for example *Lene* ≈ *Lono'/Lena*, *Beybayan* ≈ *Baybayan*, *Pamulew* ≈ *Pamulaw*). Ask Matigsalug elders.
7. **The salimbal and the Ulahingan ship.** The idea that they are the same is my inference. Do not state it as fact.
8. **Who is Apu Tulalang?** He is called "leader of all Apu" in Matigsalug prayer [2]. How he relates to Tuwaang (Manuvu) or Tolalang (San Fernando and Arakan) [29] is unclear.
9. **Magbebaye.** Is she a female deity [2], or is "Magbavaye" something Manama gave for self-governance [16]? Check with the community.
10. **Instruments.**
    - Matigsalug use of the **kuglung and saluray** is well supported [3].
    - **Agung, drum, kubing:** the detailed descriptions are of the Agusan Manobo of about 1910 [5] or of the Tigwa and Bukidnon [4]. Matigsalug-specific use was not confirmed.
    - **Kulintang** is unsupported.
    - The Brandeis 1995 paper [4] was read only as a summary.
11. **Datu Gawilan's name.** The community text says the 1975 rebellion was led by "Datu Lorenzo Gawilan, Jr.," mentions "the late Datu Lorenzo Gawilan," and calls "Datu Lorenzo Gawilan, Sr." mayor of Kitaotao [1]. Senior and Junior may be mixed up.
12. **Leader names in news.** SunStar names "Datu Carlito Guinto" (2017) and "Carlito Benito" (2019) as deputy leader [16][17]. These are probably the same person, so do not print either name without checking.
13. **The Agyu text.** The beat summaries come from secondary web retellings [12][13] that trace back to Manuel's 1963 recording and Eugenio (2001), *Philippine Folk Literature: The Epics*. Check the beats against Eugenio or Manuel in a library. One summary places the recording in Davao City [12]. Confirm this.
14. **Weaving.** "Matigsalog fabric weaving" is described as dying out [1]. It is not clear whether any weaving survives.
15. **Coordinates.** The DENR description gives only one latitude, 7°45′ [1]. The marker at 7.6° N, 125.2° E is my own estimate.
